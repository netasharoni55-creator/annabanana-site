THE ARTIST

ANNA BANANA

Working at the intersection of acrylic painting, print transfer, and macro photography, my practice examines texture as landscape.

I've been painting with acrylic for years — long before this project began. Photography came later, almost as a hobby, until I discovered what happens when you photograph a painting up close: the camera reveals an effect the eye alone doesn't catch, a second life the paint takes on once it's seen through a lens.

That discovery shaped everything that followed. I found myself drawn to the glossy sheen of printed posters — the way ink sits on paper, catching light — and to the meticulous precision of Japanese printmaking, where every line and texture is considered down to the smallest detail. Both pushed me toward monoprinting.

The process is a form of monoprinting: acrylic is applied between two surfaces — paper, sometimes cardboard, always varying — which are then pressed together and pulled apart. The texture of the surface itself becomes part of the image: its grain, its weight, its resistance all shape how the paint cracks, ridges, and pulls apart. What's left behind is never fully controlled. Acrylic has body — a viscous consistency — and it's this that allows it to hold texture as the surfaces pull apart. And it's the photograph that lets it truly come alive.

It's this in-between moment, where paint stops being paint and starts resembling tissue, capillary, or mineral formation, that I'm after. Self-taught, I've spent the past few years developing this process, photographing each result in extreme close-up until the material reveals a structure entirely its own.

Each piece begins in the randomness of thick paint and two surfaces — and becomes something impossible to stop looking at.
